Roots and land

The teachings this map stands on, and what it asks of the people who use it: care for the land that carries us.

Rooted in this land

gadugi

Cherokee
working together for others' good · said ga-doo-gee

This map sits on the homelands of the Aniyunwiya, the Principal People, the people of Kituwah, still home to the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians. As Cherokee elder and linguist Thomas N. Belt describes it, gadugi means always looking to benefit others: joining together and working together for one another's good. It reaches into the cycles of life and ceremony, and even into how living things work, in unison, each sustaining the other. Each rating is a small part of that work: one woman's experience, offered so the next woman can go out knowing more and fearing less.

orenda

Haudenosaunee
the power every living being holds

Orenda comes from the Haudenosaunee peoples, and is offered here by someone learning her own family's Haudenosaunee line. It names the power every living being holds. Fear takes some of that power away by narrowing where women go and how fully they live in their own bodies. This map exists to give some of it back, together.

Both words are offered with respect, by someone who is not Cherokee and is still learning her Haudenosaunee roots. The meaning of gadugi here follows Thomas N. Belt (Cherokee Nation) in Lisa J. Lefler and Thomas N. Belt, Sounds of Tohi: Cherokee Health and Well-Being in Southern Appalachia (University of Alabama Press), shared with this project by a member of the community. Both words remain open to review by Cherokee and Haudenosaunee knowledge holders, and will change if they ask.

The same spirit, out on the trail

Caring for the land

Gadugi, as Thomas N. Belt describes it, reaches past people and into how living things work together, each one sustaining the other. And tohi, the Kituwah word for health, cannot be pulled apart from the land it comes from. So the land belongs in what we are looking after here. These paths carry us, and many of them are still mending after Helene. Picking up what gets left behind is the same work as rating a place: small labor, offered for whoever comes next.

A cared-for place is a safer place

  • Neglect reads as riskIn one study, people shown litter in a park expected more trouble there and were likelier to believe crime was getting worse. The litter changed nothing about the park. It changed what people believed about it. Medway, Parker and Roper, Journal of Environmental Psychology, 2016 ↗
  • Trash hides real hazardsBroken glass, bottles and dumped metal on a trail or in a creek are injuries waiting on a dog, a child or a bare hand. Dumping also collects where nobody is watching, which tends to be the same stretch that already feels isolated.
  • Care is visiblePlaces are safer where people look out for one another, and a clean trailhead is that care in plain sight: proof somebody comes by here, and recently. Why together works
  • This is what we came forTime outside is tied to better health and wellbeing. Keeping these places whole is how that stays available to the next woman, and to her kids.

Ways to leave it better

  1. Pack out everything you broughtGel wrappers, tape, tissues, water bottles, dog waste bags, including the one you meant to collect on the way back. If the can at the trailhead is full, the trash rides home with you.
  2. Take one more than your ownKeep a grocery bag in the car and pick up one piece that isn't yours each time out. Across a county of runners, that is a cleanup every single day. Gloves if there's glass.
  3. Stay on the trailCutting switchbacks and walking around mud widens the path and starts the erosion that closes trails later. Go through the puddle, not around it.
  4. Leave what you findNo picking wildflowers or ramps, no carving, and no stacking rocks pulled from a creek. Those stones are somebody's habitat, and a cairn can send the next hiker the wrong way.
  5. Let wildlife stay wildKeep your distance, leash where it's posted, and feed nothing. Food left at a trailhead is how a bear learns to meet people there.
  6. Report dumping, or join a cleanupReport littering or a dump site to NCDOT's Swat-A-Litterbug at 1-800-331-5864 or ncdot.gov/litter ↗. RiverLink ↗ and Asheville GreenWorks ↗ run river and trail cleanups, and RiverLink lends out gloves, bags and grabbers if you would rather clean a stretch you already love, on your own time.

Reading gadugi and tohi out onto the land this way is this project's own, not Dr. Belt's or Dr. Lefler's, and it is part of the cultural review still underway. The practical half follows the Leave No Trace principles, kept by the Leave No Trace Center for Outdoor Ethics and used by the National Park Service, written here for a two-mile loop instead of a backcountry trip.
