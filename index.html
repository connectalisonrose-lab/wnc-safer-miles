WNC Safer Miles

A community map of parks, greenways and trails in Buncombe, Henderson and Jackson counties, scored on what helps women feel safer running or walking alone.

For all women, trans women included, and for nonbinary, gender-expansive and LGBTQ+ people who have ever felt unsafe out alone.

More about this pilot

This is a pilot, and the scores are starting estimates. A high score means fewer known risk factors, not a guarantee of safety. The starting scores come from a review of public sources, such as park and county pages, local news and Google Maps listings, not from site visits, and they are waiting for local women to confirm or correct them. Anyone can rate a place from its panel; it takes about 20 seconds and asks for no name. Use this as one input alongside your own judgment. This map is not an emergency service; if you are in danger, call 911.

Safety tips

Going out with a plan

None of this is a list of what you owe anyone. The responsibility for harm always belongs to the person who causes it. These are practices women here already use to keep the miles they love, gathered in one place so no one has to learn them alone. Take what fits and leave the rest.

Before you go

  • Tell one person your route and when you'll be back, and text them when you're done.
  • Check this map for cell signal before you pick a route. Some trails here lose signal minutes from the trailhead. Charge your phone and download an offline map.
  • Plan for the light. Dusk comes early under trees. If you'll be out near sunset, start at the open end of a route and finish there.
  • Check the season. Gates and hours change through the year, and on state game lands like Bent Creek hunting is allowed in season. Wear something bright in fall and winter.
  • Leave the car ready: parked facing out near the lot entrance, nothing visible inside, key within reach.

While you're out

  • Keep one ear free, or skip music on quiet stretches. Hearing is how you get the extra seconds.
  • Trust the feeling. If a person, a car or a stretch of trail makes you uneasy, turn around, leave or cut the run short. You never need a reason that would satisfy someone else.
  • Stay alert at the edges. The parking lot and the first and last quarter mile are where most unwanted encounters start.
  • If someone follows you, go toward people: a dog park, a field, a staffed building, another runner. Say out loud what is happening.
  • Vary your routes and times so your pattern is harder to predict.

Your phone and apps

  • Set up emergency SOS today. Most phones call 911 and alert your contacts with a few presses of the side button. Practice once so you know it.
  • Share live location with one trusted person for the length of your run, then turn it off.
  • Lock down fitness apps. Make activities private or followers-only, hide the start and end near home, and opt out of public heatmaps. Researchers at NC State showed public heatmaps can still reveal where frequent runners live.

Together is a strategy

  • Run with someone when you can. A partner changes which routes are open to you. Local running stores can point you to women's group runs.
  • Nod to other women on the trail. Being seen, and seeing, is part of what makes a place safe.
  • Rate the places you use. Your rating turns this map from estimates into something the next woman can trust.

If you share these trails

Most people on these paths mean no harm. A few habits make that obvious to a woman who can't yet tell.

  • Call out before you pass, and pass wide.
  • Don't match pace behind someone. Pass, or fall back.
  • Don't stop her to talk. Headphones in and eyes forward is an answer.
  • If you see someone being followed or bothered, walk alongside, ask if she's okay, and stay until she says she is.

If today doesn't feel right

Not going is a choice, not a failure. If a place or a day doesn't feel safe, that feeling is information worth trusting. Some other ways to get your miles:

  • Pick a busier, staffed place for today. Filter the map to Strong, or choose somewhere with staff during open hours.
  • Go with someone. Local running stores and clubs host group runs, and a friend or a dog changes which routes feel open.
  • Move indoors. A YMCA, YWCA, gym or community recreation center gives you a track or treadmill with people and staff around.
  • Stay close to home. A shorter daylight loop in your own neighborhood, or a route you know well, still counts.
  • Move at home. Stairs, a walking or yoga video, or stretching on the floor keeps the habit without the trip.
  • Rest. A day off is part of training, and so is listening to your body.

After a scare

A frightening moment can stay in the body after it's over. A racing heart, shaking or feeling numb are normal responses. These are options for the hour afterward; use what helps.

  • Get somewhere safer first: a lit lot, a staffed building, other people, or your locked car.
  • Slow the exhale. Breathe out longer than you breathe in, a few times. A longer exhale helps your body settle.
  • Come back to right now. Name five things you can see and three you can hear, and press your feet into the ground.
  • Hold something. Keys, a smooth stone or anything with texture in your hand gives your body something steady to focus on.
  • Reach out. Text someone you trust, or call or text an advocate below. You don't have to explain it well.
  • Later, if and when you want: rate the place so the next woman is warned, and try a different option next time. There's no rush, and no wrong way to recover.

Rooted in this land

gadugi

Tsalagi (Cherokee)
working together · said ga-doo-gee

This map sits on Tsalagi homelands, still home to the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians. Gadugi is the Cherokee tradition of people turning out together to help a neighbor plant, harvest or get through hard times. Each rating is a small part of that work: one woman's experience, offered so the next woman can go out knowing more and fearing less.

orenda

Haudenosaunee
the power every living being holds

Orenda comes from the Haudenosaunee peoples, and is offered here by someone learning her own family's Haudenosaunee line. It names the power every living being holds. Fear takes some of that power away by narrowing where women go and how fully they live in their own bodies. This map exists to give some of it back, together.

Both words are offered with respect, by someone who is not Cherokee and is still learning her Haudenosaunee roots. They are awaiting review by Cherokee and Haudenosaunee knowledge holders, and will change if they ask.

How this map works

How the score works

Each place gets 1–5 on six factors, weighted toward what matters most when you're alone:

  • Foot traffic, 25%
  • Open sightlines, 20%
  • Cell signal, 15%
  • Staff or ranger presence, 15%
  • Lighting, 15%
  • Visible parking, 10%

The result is scaled to 100. Women's ratings then blend in: one rating can move a score by up to about 13 points, enough to change the band of a place near a cutoff, and the more ratings a place has, the more they count, up to 40% of the score. Any rating of 1 or 2 is also flagged on the place itself as a warning.

No police data

This map does not use police or crime data. Incident counts mostly show where policing happens and what gets reported, most harassment on trails is never reported, and crime maps can make a place look more dangerous, or safer, than it feels to a woman alone. The one exception is a serious violent incident at a specific place, such as an assault or an attack on a trail, confirmed by an official notice or reliable local news. After review, it may appear on that place as a dated alert with its source. Alerts never change a score, and they come down 12 months after the incident.

What this map won't do

It rates places, never people. It won't show who runs where, when anyone goes, or live locations, so it can't be used to find a woman alone. Ratings are saved without names and the page shows only scores, counts and tags, never who rated.
