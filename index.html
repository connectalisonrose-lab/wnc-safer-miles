Safety tips

Going out with a plan

These are options, not rules. Women here use different ones depending on the place, the hour and the day. The responsibility for harm always belongs to the person who causes it. Take what fits and leave the rest.

Before you go

  • Tell one person where you're going and when you'll be back, and text them when you're done.
  • Check sunset. Loops take longer than planned, and the places marked Go prepared change most after dark.
  • Charge your phone and download an offline map for forest trails like Bent Creek, where signal drops.
  • Park facing out, near the lot entrance or under a light, so leaving is quick.

While you're moving

  • Keep one ear free. Hearing what's behind you matters more on wooded stretches than open loops.
  • Vary your routes and times so your pattern is harder to predict.
  • Trust the feeling. Turning around, crossing over or cutting a run short needs no explanation.
  • Head toward people, a staffed building or a road if something feels wrong.

Your phone and apps

  • Set up emergency SOS today. Most phones call 911 and alert your contacts with a few presses of the side button. Practice once so you know it.
  • Share live location with one trusted person for the length of your run, then turn it off.
  • Lock down fitness apps. Make activities private or followers-only, hide the start and end near home, and opt out of public heatmaps. Researchers at NC State showed public heatmaps can still reveal where frequent runners live.

How the score works

Each place gets 1–5 on six factors, weighted toward what matters most when you're alone:

  • Foot traffic, 25%
  • Open sightlines, 20%
  • Cell signal, 15%
  • Staff or ranger presence, 15%
  • Lighting, 15%
  • Visible parking, 10%

The result is scaled to 100. Women's ratings then blend in: one rating shifts the score a little, and the more ratings a place has, the more they count, up to 40% of the score. Any rating of 1 or 2 is also flagged on the place itself as a warning.

About police data

Asheville publishes incident data, but it covers city limits only, and the Sheriff's Office covers the rest of the county. Incident counts reflect where policing happens and what gets reported. Most harassment on trails never reaches a report. So this layer will show context next to each place and stay out of the score.

What this map won't do

It rates places, never people. It won't show who runs where, when anyone goes, or live locations, so it can't be used to find a woman alone. Ratings are saved without names and the page shows only scores, counts and tags, never who rated.

Before you go

Running alone, well

None of this is a list of what you owe anyone. The responsibility for harm sits with whoever causes it. These are the practices women here already use to keep the miles they love, gathered in one place so no one has to learn them alone.

Set it up

  • Tell one person your route and when you'll be back. A text is enough. Many watches and phones can share your live location with that one person, and only them.
  • Check this map for cell signal before you pick a route. Some trails here lose signal within a few minutes of the trailhead.
  • Plan for the light. Dusk comes fast in the mountains and earlier than you expect under trees. If you'll be out near sunset, start at the open end of a route and finish there.
  • Leave the car ready: nothing visible inside, key where you can reach it, parked where you can see the lot from the trail if you can.

While you're out

  • Keep one ear open, or run without music on quiet stretches. Hearing is how you get the extra seconds.
  • Trust the feeling. If a person, a car or a stretch of trail makes you uneasy, change your plan. Turn around, leave, cut the run short. You never need a reason that would satisfy someone else.
  • Keep your head up through the parking lot and the first and last quarter mile. The trailhead is where most unwanted encounters start.
  • If someone is following you, go toward people, not away from them: a dog park, a sports field, a staffed building, another runner. Say out loud what is happening.

Together is a strategy

  • A running partner changes everything about a route, including which ones are open to you. Women's run groups in Asheville meet on the greenways most weeks; ask at a local running store.
  • Nod to other women on the trail. Being seen, and seeing, is part of what makes a place safe.
  • Rate the places you use. Your rating is what turns this from one person's guesses into something the next woman can trust.

If something happens

You decide what counts as something, and you decide what to do about it. These lines are free, confidential, and staffed around the clock by people who will follow your lead.

  • Emergency: 911
  • Our VOICE (sexual violence, Buncombe County), 24-hour crisis line: 828-255-7576
  • Helpmate (domestic violence, Buncombe County), 24-hour helpline: 828-254-0516
  • National Sexual Assault Hotline (RAINN): 800-656-4673

Being followed, watched or harassed is reason enough to call. You do not need to have been hurt.

If you share these trails

Most people on these paths mean no harm. A few habits make that obvious to a woman who can't yet tell:

  • Call out before you pass, and pass wide.
  • Don't match pace behind someone. Pass, or fall back.
  • Don't stop her to talk. Headphones in and eyes forward is an answer.
  • If you see someone being followed or bothered, step in with your presence: walk alongside, ask if she's okay, stay until she says she is.
