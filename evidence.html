Evidence and method

Where the scores come from, the research behind them, and what this map deliberately will not do.

Why this matters

What women already know

If you have changed your route, your hour or what you wear to feel safer outside, the research says most women have too.

  • 53%of U.S. women say they would be afraid to walk alone at night within a mile of home, about twice the share of men (26%).Gallup, 2023 ↗
  • 92%of women runners have safety concerns about running outside, and 51% fear being physically attacked, compared with 28% of men.adidas and White Ribbon survey, 2023 ↗

The six factors behind each score come from decades of research on how places feel to women. See the research

How this map works

How the score works

Each place gets 1–5 on six factors, weighted toward what matters most when you're alone:

  • Foot traffic, 25%
  • Open sightlines, 20%
  • Cell signal, 15%
  • Staff or ranger presence, 15%
  • Lighting, 15%
  • Visible parking, 10%

The result is scaled to 100. Women's ratings then blend in: one rating can move a score by up to about 13 points, enough to change the band of a place near a cutoff, and the more ratings a place has, the more they count, up to 40% of the score. Any rating of 1 or 2 is also flagged on the place itself as a warning.

No police data

This map does not use police or crime data. Incident counts mostly show where policing happens and what gets reported, most harassment on trails is never reported, and crime maps can make a place look more dangerous, or safer, than it feels to a woman alone. The one exception is a serious violent incident at a specific place, such as an assault or an attack on a trail, confirmed by an official notice or reliable local news. After review, it may appear on that place as a dated alert with its source. Alerts never change a score, and they come down 12 months after the incident.

What this map won't do

It rates places, never people. It won't show who runs where, when anyone goes, or live locations, so it can't be used to find a woman alone. Ratings are saved without names and the page shows only scores, counts and tags, never who rated.

The research behind it

Women's safety audits began in Toronto in 1989, created by METRAC (the Metropolitan Toronto Action Committee on Violence Against Women and Children). Women walk a place together and note what makes it feel unsafe, such as poor lighting or missing signs. UN-Habitat now shares the method as a tool for cities. UN-Habitat guide ↗

Bonnie Fisher and Jack Nasar found that fear runs highest where someone could hide, where you can't see far ahead and where there is no easy way out (Environment and Behavior, 1992). That is why open sightlines carry a fifth of every score. Study summary ↗

Harassment is common, too: 84% of women in a Runner's World survey of more than 5,000 runners said they had been harassed while running. Via CBC News ↗

This map turns those same questions into six factors, then asks local women to confirm or correct them. Your ratings are part of the evidence.

Health, in two languages

Cherokee knowledge comes first here. Tohi (ᏙᎯ, said tow-hee) is the Kituwah word for health. In Sounds of Tohi, Cherokee elder and linguist Thomas N. Belt and medical anthropologist Lisa J. Lefler describe health that cannot be pulled apart from land, language and community. As Belt explains gadugi, working for one another's good reaches all the way into how living things work together.

Western health science arrived at part of this in the biopsychosocial model (George Engel, 1977): body, mind and community shape one another. This map draws on all three.

  • Body. Your body's read on a place is information, and that is what a rating records. Exercising with others and time outdoors both show measurable benefits (Yorks and colleagues, 2017; White and colleagues, 2019).
  • Mind. Fear narrows where women go and how fully they live in their bodies, the loss orenda names. Open sightlines and ways out ease it (Fisher and Nasar, 1992).
  • Community. Places are safer where people look out for one another (Sampson, Raudenbush and Earls, 1997). Each rating is that care, offered at the scale of the land.

Linking tohi and gadugi to the biopsychosocial model is this project's own reading, not Dr. Belt's or Dr. Lefler's, and it is part of the cultural review still underway.
